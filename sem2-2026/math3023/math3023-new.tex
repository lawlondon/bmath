\section{The Vector Space $\mathbb{R}^n$}
\newthought{A vector space} $V$ on $\mathbb{F}$ is the set $V$ of objects named \emph{vectors} and a
field\footnote{\ refer: Algebra}
$\mathbb{F}$ of objects name \emph{scalars}, with two operations \emph{vector addition} and \emph{scalar
multiplication} defined:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Vector Addition: $+$: $V \times V \to V$
  \item Scalar Multiplication: $\cdot$: $\mathbb{F} \times V \to V$
\end{enumerate}
That satisfies the axioms of a vector space. Namely that vector addition and scalar multiplication are both
commutative, associative,
and distributive, and that there exists additive and multiplicative identities and inverses.

\newthought{The vector space $\mathbb{R}^n$} is the set $V = \{ (x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n) : x_i \in
\mathbb{R}\}$ over the real numbers.
Elements of $V$ are said to be $n$-tuples of real numbers. Vector addition is defined to be the
coordinate-wise addition of elements
of $V$:
\[(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n) + (x'_0, x'_1, \ldots, x'_n) = (x_0 + x'_0, x_1 + x'_1, \ldots, x_n + x'_n)\]
with the usual arithmetic inherited from the real numbers. Scalar multiplication is defined as the
distributive multiplication of each
coordinate by some $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$:
\[\alpha \cdot (x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n) = (\alpha \cdot x_0, \alpha \cdot x_1, \ldots, \alpha \cdot x_n)\]
In two and three dimensions, $n$-tuples of numbers can be thought of as a quantity with \emph{magnitude and
direction}, and arrow
from the origin to $(x,y,z)$, however to not lose generality in higher dimensions it is best just to think of
them as ordered lists of
real numbers, with arithmetic defined above.

\newthought{The dot product} of two vectors is defined:
\[\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_{n=1}^{n}a_i b_i\]
for two vectors $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. It is also known as the scalar or inner product.
An alternative
formulation of the dot product is:
\[\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\ \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta\]
where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, and $\|\mathbf{a}\|$ is the magnitude, or
length, of $\mathbf{a}$.
Thus, two orthogonal vectors will have a dot product of 0 (since $\cos(\pi / 2) = 0$) and two parallel
vectors will have a dot product
equal to the product of their magnitudes. Notice
\[\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 \implies \|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a}}\]
The dot product can be thought of geometrically as a measure of how much of $\mathbf{a}$ \emph{goes in} the
direction of $\mathbf{b}$.
A handy application of this fact is determining the $x, y$, and $z$ components of a vector $\mathbf{a}$, by
taking its dot product with
the standard basis vectors\footnote{\ $\mathbf{i} = (1,0,0),\ \mathbf{j} = (0,1,0),\ \mathbf{k} = (0,0,1)$}
$\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}$, and
$\mathbf{k}$, respectively.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{right-hand-rule.png}
  \caption{The right hand rule is the convention used for the positive direction $\mathbf{n}$ in $\mathbf{a}
  \times \mathbf{b}$.}
  \label{fig:right-hand-rule}
\end{wrapfigure}

\newthought{The cross product} of two vectors $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is defined:
\[\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = (\|\mathbf{a}\|\ \|\mathbf{b}\| \sin\theta)\cdot \mathbf{n}\]
where $\theta$ is the angle between them and $\mathbf{n}$ is a unit vector perpendicular to the plane
containing $\mathbf{a}$ and
$\mathbf{b}$,
whose direction is given by the right hand rule (\cref{fig:right-hand-rule}). A vector perpendicular to a
plane is said to be \emph{normal}
to that plane.

An easy way to calculate the cross product is to build the $3 \times 3$ matrix with first row
$[\mathbf{i}\ \mathbf{j}\ \mathbf{k}]$,
second row $[\mathbf{a}]$, and third row $[\mathbf{b}]$, and take its determinant.

\[
  \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} =
  \begin{vmatrix}
    \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\
    a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\
    b_1 & b_2 & b_3
  \end{vmatrix}
\]
\[
  \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = (a_2b_3 - a_3b_2)\mathbf{i} + (a_3b_1 - a_1b_3)\mathbf{j} + (a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)\mathbf{k}
\]
Once you know a normal vector to a plane (usually by taking the cross product of two vectors in that plane)
and one point in the plane,
it is easy to determine an equation for that plane, since the dot product of \emph{any} vector in the plane
with the normal
vector will be 0.
\[\mathbf{n} \cdot (x,y,z) = n_x x + n_y y + n_z z + d = 0\]
Simply plug in the $(x,y,z)$ that you know to determine the correct value of $d$.

\section{Vector Valued Functions}
\newthought{A function} $f$ is said to \emph{vector valued} if it is a mapping $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$.
In other words, vector valued
function take a real number and assign a vector.
\[\mathbf{r}(t) = (x_1(t), x_2(t), \ldots, x_n(t))\]
The functions $x_1(t), \ldots, x_n(t)$ are called the \emph{coordinate functions} of $\mathbf{r}$.

A common example of a vector valued
function is $\mathbf{r}(t)=(t,f(t))$ where $f:\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ and $t\in[a,b] : a,b\in\mathbb{R}$.
This function describes a
\emph{parametrisation} of $f$ and points from the origin to every point of $f$ as $t$ varies in its domain,
as if the tip were moving
along $f$. It is helpful to imagine $t$ as representing time. This will be covered in more detail in Chapter 7.

\section{Functions of Several Variables}
\newthought{A function} $f$ is a \emph{multivariable} function if it is a mapping from $\mathbb{R}^n \to
\mathbb{R}$. These functions take a
vector and assign to it a real number. The coordinates of the input vector can be thought of as several
independent variables.
\[f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) = n\]
where $n$ is some combination of the variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$. Just like we can plot a function of one
variable $f: D \to R$ by defining
the line
$l = \{(x,f(x)) : x \in D\}$
as the set of all points that satisfy $(x,f(x))$, we can define an $n+1$ dimensional surface of a function
$f: D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \to R \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ of $n$ variables by
\[S = \{(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n,f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)) : x_1, \ldots, x_n \in D\}\]
Allowing an $n+1$ dimensional alien to understand its plot. The only applications where this is useful to
humans is to plot $z = f(x,y)$
or every point $(x,y,z)$ that satisfies $f(x,y,z) = k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{R}$ in three dimensions.

\newthought{Fields} are mappings $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, usually $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^m$.
They can be thought of as
assigning to every point in space a vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$. A common example is a force or electromagnetic
field. A scalar field is a
special multivariable function of three variables that assigns to every point in space a number, like
temperature or pressure. These are
covered in Chapter 8.

\section{Functions of One Variable}
\newthought{A function} $f$ approaches a limit $l$ near $a$ if for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists some
$\delta > 0$, such
that if $ 0 < |x-a| < \delta$,
then $|f(x)-l|<\epsilon$.

This is a precise way of writing that if a function approaches a value $l$ near $a$, then no matter how small
of an interval we
draw around $l$, we will always
be able to draw an interval around $a$, where $f(x)$ is within that interval of $l$. It will become clear in
the examples why
this definition matches the intuitive
understanding of what a limit should be.

\newthought{Consider} $f(x)=x^2$ (\cref{fig:x2}). Obviously it approaches 0 near 0 (and $a^2$ near $a$), but
how would we show that?
We have to decide how to ensure that $|x^2-a^2| < \epsilon$ whenever $|x-a| < \delta$. We want
\[|x^2-a^2| = |x-a| \cdot |x+a| < \epsilon\]
The factor $|x+a|$ is obviously the problem, but as long as we know \emph{an} upper bound $M$ for it, we can
just ensure that $|x-a|<1/M$.
If we take $|x+a|<1$, then
\[|x+a| = |(x-a)+2a| \le |x-a| + |2a| < 1 + 2|a|\]
Now, we just need
\[|x-a| < \dfrac{\epsilon}{1+2|a|}\]
for $|x^2-a^2| < \epsilon$. So, let $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta =
\min\left(1,\dfrac{\epsilon}{1+2|a|}\right)$. Then $\delta \le 1$,
so $|x-a|<1$ and $|x+a|<1+2|a|$. Consider $|x^2-a^2| = |x-a| \cdot |x+a|$. Substituting our bounds,
$|x^2-a^2| = \delta \cdot (1+2|a|) \le \dfrac{\epsilon}{1+2|a|} \cdot (1+2|a|) = \epsilon$.

So for every $\epsilon$, there is an interval around $a$, equal to $a \pm
\min\left(1,\dfrac{\epsilon}{1+2|a|}\right)$ that will
ensure $|f(x)-l| < \epsilon$.

This function oscillates wildly as $x \to 0$ and so does not approach any value at $x=0$. The intuition for
this fact is that no
matter how small we make an
interval containing $0$, we will always be able to choose a large enough natural number $n$, such that there is a point
$a=\dfrac{2}{\pi(1+4n)}$ where $\sin{a}=1$, and
a point $b=\dfrac{2}{\pi(3+4n)}$ where $\sin{b}=-1$.

Assume for contradiction that $\lim_{x \to 0}\sin{1/x}=l$. Let $\epsilon=1/2$ and $\delta > 0$. Now choose a
sufficiently large
natural number $n$, such that
$a=\dfrac{2}{\pi(1+4n)}< \delta$. Then, there will also be the point $b=\dfrac{2}{\pi(3+4n)} < a < \delta$. Then,
\begin{alignat*}{2}
  |f(a)-l| & = |1-l|   &  & < \epsilon = \dfrac{1}{2} \\
  |f(b)-l| & = | -1-l| &  & < \epsilon = \dfrac{1}{2}
\end{alignat*}

By the triangle inequality
\[
  |1+1| = |(1-l)+(1+l)| \le |1-l| + |1+l| < \dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2} = 1 \implies 2 < 1
\]
Which is a contradiction. So our original assumption that the limit exists and equals $l$ must be wrong.\qed\

Perhaps counterintuitively at the moment, this function approaches $0$ at $a$.
Recall the definition of a limit, the function $f$ \emph{approaches} $l$ at $a$ if for every
$\epsilon > 0, \ \exists \delta > 0, \text{ with }0< |x-a|<\delta, \text{ and } |f(x)-l|< \epsilon$.
We don't actually care what $f(a)$ is, since the difference between $x$ and $a$ will always be greater
than $0$, the value at $f(a)$ is irrelevant.

Let $\epsilon > 0$ be given. Choose a sufficiently large
natural number $n$ such that $1/n < \epsilon$. The only numbers $x$ where $|f(x)-0|<\epsilon$ could be false are
\[
  \frac12,\ \frac13,\ \frac23,\ \frac14,\ \frac34,\ \frac15,\ \frac25,\ \mathellipsis,\ \frac{1}{n},\ \mathellipsis,\ \frac{n-1}{n}
\]
the set of which we can call $S$.

However many of these numbers there are, there are only finitely many, and so one of them is closest to $a$. That is,
$|p/q-a|$ is smallest for one specific $p/q \in S$. If $a$ is in $S$, then just consider all the elements of $S \ne a$.
If we chose $\delta$ to be this smallest distance then for all $x$ satisfying $0 < |x-a| < \delta$, $x$ is
guaranteed not
to be in $S$, and therefore $|f(x)-0|<\epsilon$, which completes our proof.\qed\

There is no need to give $\delta$ in terms of $\epsilon$, since there is nowhere in the definition
$\mathellipsis \forall \epsilon, \ \exists \delta \mathellipsis$ that says each $\epsilon$ needs a
\emph{unique} $\delta$.

\subsection{Divergence to, and Limits at, Infinity}
\newthought{The definition of a limit} can be modified to handle divergence to infinity. A function $l$
approaches $\infty$ near $a$
if for every $M > 0$, there exists a $\delta$ such that, whenever $|x-a| < \delta$, $f(x) > M$. No matter how
large $M$ is, there will
always be an interval we can draw around $a$, such that $f(x) > M$.

\newthought{Limits at infinity} have a similar modified definition. A function $f$ approaches a limit $l$
near $\infty$ if for every
$\epsilon > 0$, there exists an $M > 0$ such that if $x > M$, $|f(x)-l| < \epsilon$. For every interval
around the limit we draw, there is
always a point on the number line that ensures we are within that interval.

\subsection{One Sided Limits}

\newthought{One-sided limits} are exactly the same as the full limit, except that $x$ is only considered to
be approaching $a$ from
the left or the right. The full limit only exists if both one-sided limits equal, but it is possible for
either one-sided limit to exist
independently.

For example, define (\cref{fig:one-sided-limit}) \[f(x) \coloneqq \dfrac{d|x|}{dx} =
  \begin{cases}
    -1 & x<0 \\
    1 & x>0
\end{cases}\]
If we examine what $f$ does as $x \to 0$ from the left, its quite obvious that it approaches -1, and 1 from
the right. We say that
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{x \to 0^-}f(x) &= -1 \\
  \lim_{x \to 0^+}f(x) &= 1
\end{align*}
but the full limit:\[\lim_{x \to 0}f(x)\]is not defined.

\section{Techniques for Evaluating Limits}
\newthought{The limit laws} allow for breaking up complicated limits in several trivial to solve limits.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\lim_{x \to a}(f+g)(x) = \lim_{x \to a}f(x) + \lim_{x \to a}g(x)$
  \item $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) \cdot g(x) = \lim_{x \to a}f(x) \cdot \lim_{x \to a}g(x)$
  \item $\lim_{x \to a}c \cdot f(x) = c \cdot \lim_{x \to a}f(x)$
  \item $\lim_{x \to a}\left(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right) = \dfrac{\lim_{x \to a}f(x)}{\lim_{x \to a}g(x)}$
  \item If $\lim_{x \to a}g(x) = l$ and $f$ is continuous at $l$, then $\lim_{x \to a}f(g(x)) = f(l)$
\end{enumerate}

\newthought{The squeeze theorem} states that if $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to a}g(x) = l =
\lim_{x \to a}h(x)$, then
$\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = l$.

\newthought{L'H$\hat{\text{o}}$pital's Rule} states that if $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, and $\lim_{x \to
a}f(x) = \lim_{x \to
a}g(x) = 0$ or $\pm \infty$,
then $\lim_{x \to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a}\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$.

Proofs of these assertions are a bit beyond the scope of these notes.

\section{Limits of Multivariable Functions}
\newthought{For limits} of multivariable functions, one must consider every possible path to $a$. Just like
with functions of one variable,
where we require the left and right sided limits to equal, for multivariable functions, we require all paths
to be equal. For example:
\[\lim_{(x,y) \to (0,0)}f(x,y) = \dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\]
Consider the path $x=y$:
\[\lim_{(x,y) \to (x,x)}f(x,y) = \lim_{(x,y) \to (x,x)}\dfrac{x^2}{x^2+x^2} = \dfrac{1}{2}\]
Now, consider the path $x=0$:
\[\lim_{(x,y) \to (0,y)}f(x,y) = \lim_{(x,y) \to (0,y)}\dfrac{0}{y^2} = 0 \ne \dfrac{1}{2}\]
Thus, the limit does not exist.

The technique for solving limits of multivariable functions is to investigate a few paths to observe their
behaviour. If the limit exists
then a symbolic proof must be given to cover all possible paths. Otherwise, one example will suffice.
Evaluating the limit uses similar
techniques to single variable limits.

Consider\[\lim_{(x,y) \to (0,0)}\dfrac{x^2+x+xy+y}{x+y}\]Naively evaluating $(0,0)$ produces an indeterminate
$\dfrac{0}{0}$, but
L'H$\hat{\text{o}}$pital's rule is not defined for multivariable functions. However if we factorise the numerator:
\[\lim_{(x,y) \to (0,0)}\dfrac{x^2+x+xy+y}{x+y} = \lim_{(x,y) \to (0,0)}\dfrac{(x+y)(x+1)}{x+y} = \lim_{(x,y)
\to (0,0)}x+1 = 1\]
We did not make any assumptions about the path taken by $x$ and $y$ to $(0,0)$, and so this symbolic proof
remains true for every
possible path.

\section{Continuity}
\newthought{A function is continuous} if $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = f(a)$. Common continuous functions include
\begin{enumerate}
  \item All polynomials $p_o + p_1 x + p_2 x^2 + \cdots + p_n x^n$.
  \item All rational functions $R(x) = \dfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ except where $Q(x)=0$.
  \item $\sin x$ and $\cos x$.
  \item $e^x$ and $\ln x$ (on its domain $x \in (0,\infty)$).
  \item $x^{\alpha}$ for $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ and $x>0$.
  \item Linear combinations and products of continuous functions are continuous.
  \item Ratios of continuous functions are continuous, provided the denominator is not 0.
  \item $f(g(x))$ is continuous when $g(x)$ is continuous, provided the codomain of $g$ is the domain of $f$.
\end{enumerate}

\newthought{For a function} $f$, define its \emph{derivative} at $a$:
\[f'(a) \coloneqq \lim_{h \to 0}\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\]

If this limit exists, the function is called \emph{differentiable}. Define

\newthought{If a function is differentiable at $a$, then it is continuous at $a$.} \emph{Proof:}
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{h \to 0}f(a+h)-f(a) &= \lim_{h \to 0}\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \cdot h \\
  &= \lim_{h \to 0}\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \cdot \lim_{h \to 0}h \\
  &= f'(a) \cdot 0 \\
  &= 0  \\
  \implies \lim_{h \to 0}f(a+h)-f(a) &= 0 \\
  \implies \lim_{x \to a}f(x) &= f(a)
\end{align*} \qed\

Note the inverse is certainly not true. The absolute value function is continuous everywhere, but not
differentiable at 0 (\cref{fig:one-sided-limit}).

\newthought{One may wish} to know more about a function and its derivatives. For example, if $d(t)$ represents
distance from an origin w.r.t time, then $d'(t)$ represents the velocity of the object w.r.t time. If
$v(t)=d'(t)$, then $v'(t)$ represents the acceleration w.r.t time. We say that acceleration is the
\emph{second derivative} of $d(t)$, written $d''(t)$. Define
\[f^{(n)} \coloneqq {(((f')')')}^{\cdots}\]
To be the $n^{th}$ derivative of $f$. This collection of derivatives are known as higher-order derivatives.

\subsection{Notation}
\newthought{For single variable functions} Newton's $f'$, $f''$, $f''\,'$, etc.\ is unambiguous. However, when partial
derivatives of functions of several variables are introduced, it will become necessary to specify which variable
the function is being differentiated with respect to. For this reason, Leibniz's operator notation is commonly used.
\begin{align*}
  \dfrac{df}{dx} &= f'(x) \\
  \dfrac{d^2 f}{dx^2} &= f''(x) \\
  \dfrac{d^n f}{dx^n} &= f^{(n)}(x)
\end{align*}

\section{Differentiating}
\newthought{Once the derivatives} of the elementary functions have been obtained, there are some crucial rules for
finding the derivatives of their products, sums, quotients and compositions. See the table for derivatives of
elementary functions.

Assuming $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, and defined on the prerequisite domains:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $[f \pm g]' = f' \pm g'$
  \item $\forall c \in \mathbb{R}, [c \cdot f]' = c \cdot f'$
  \item $[fg]' = fg' + gf'$
  \item $\left[\dfrac{f}{g}\right]' = \dfrac{1}{g^2}(gf' - fg')$
  \item $[f \circ g]' = (f' \circ g) \cdot g' $
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Inverse Functions}
\newthought{Let} $f$ be a bijective function on its domain. Then there exists a function $g$ called the \emph{inverse}
of $f$, such that $(g \circ f)(x) = x$. The inverse function of $f$ is commonly notated $f^{-1}$. This is not to be
confused with $f$'s multiplicative inverse $1/f(x)$, which will be denoted like that.

Assuming $f$ is differentiable and invertible,
\[(f^{-1})' = \dfrac{1}{f' \circ f^{-1}}\]
For example:
\[(\ln x)' = \dfrac{1}{e^{\ln x}} = \dfrac{1}{x}\]

\section{Vector Valued Functions}
\newthought{The derivative} of a vector valued function $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is
\[\mathbf{r}'(t)=(x'_1(t),x'_2(t), \ldots, x'_n(t))\]
where $x_1(t), \ldots, x_n(t)$ are the coordinate functions of $\mathbf{r}(t)$.

The same rules apply to differentiating vector valued functions as above, except
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $[\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}]' = \mathbf{u}' \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}'$
  \item $[\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}]' = \mathbf{u}' \times \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}'$
\end{enumerate}

\newthought{Recall} that if $\mathbf{r}(t)$ traces a curve $C$ in space, then $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ will be tangent to
$C$ at $t$. Define
\[\mathbf{T}(t) \coloneqq \dfrac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|}\]
to be the unit tangent vector to $C$ at $t$. Since $\mathbf{r}(t)$ encodes position along a curve $C$,
$\mathbf{r}'(t)$ encodes
the velocity at $t$ (including direction). Acceleration is given by $\mathbf{r}''(t)$, with unit acceleration given by
$\mathbf{T}'(t)$.

\section{Multivariable Functions}
\newthought{For a function} $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, we cannot meaningfully speak about the derivative of $f$
before first deciding which variable we are differentiating with respect to. Define the \emph{partial derivative} of
$f$ w.r.t $x_n$ at $\mathbf{c}$:
\[f'_{xi}(\mathbf{c})=\dfrac{\partial f(\mathbf{c})}{\partial x_i} = \lim_{h \to
0}\dfrac{f(c_{x1},\ldots,c_{xi}+h,c_{xn})-f(\mathbf{c})}{h}\]
This is calculated by treating all other $x_j \in \mathbf{c}$ as constants, and differentiating only w.r.t $x_i$. They
can then be read from derivative tables or calculated using the methods of single variable functions.

If we imagine a path along some surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ defined by $S=\{(x,y,f(x,y))\}$, fixing $y=b$ and $x=a$:
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{r}_x(x) &= (x,b,f(x,b)) \\
  \mathbf{r}_y(y) &= (a,y,f(a,y))
\end{align*}
describe curves of constant $y$ and $x$ respecively along $S$. Taking their first partial derivatives w.r.t $x$ and $y$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{r}'_x(x) &= (1,0,f'_{x}(a,b)) \\
  \mathbf{r}'_y(y) &= (0,1,f'_{y}(a,b))
\end{align*}
we arrive at two vectors perpendicular to $S$ at $(a,b,f(a,b))$, and thus they form a \emph{tangent plane} $P$ to
$S$. We can now obtain a normal vector to that plane:
\[\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{r}'_x(x) \times \mathbf{r}'_y(y) = (-f'_{x}(a,b),-f'_{y}(a,b),1)\]
Since $\mathbf{n}$ is necessarily perpendicular to $P$, its dot product with any vector in $P$ is 0, and so
it takes the form:
\[-f'_{x}(a,b)x-f'_{y}(a,b)y+z=d\]
For some $d \in \mathbb{R}$, which can be determined by using the fact that $(a,b,f(a,b)) \in P$.

\subsection{Gradient Vector and Direction Derivatives}
\newthought{Let} $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$. Define the gradient operator:
\[\Delta = \left( \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_1},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial
x_2},\ldots,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_n}  \right)\]
to be the vector operator in $\mathbb{R}^n$ of each partial derivative w.r.t $x_i$. Then $\Delta
f(\mathbf{c})$, called the gradient vector of $f$ at $\mathbf{c}$ is the $n$-dimensional vector, normal to a
level curve of $f$ at $\mathbf{c}$ that points in the direction of maximum rate of change of $f$. This
provides techniques for determining two properties of curves (if we're lucky!).

\newthought{The directional derivative} of $f(\mathbf{c})$ towards $\mathbf{u}$ is the rate of change of
$f(\mathbf{c})$ in the direction of
$\mathbf{u}$. It can be calculated by taking the dot product of a unit vector in the direction of
$\mathbf{u}$ with $\Delta f(\mathbf{c})$:
\[\text{Directional Derivative} = \Delta f(\mathbf{c}) \cdot \dfrac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}\]

\newthought{If $S$ is a level surface}, i.e.\ it is of the form $S = \{f(\mathbf{c}) = k\}$, then $\Delta
f(\mathbf{c})$ has the same dimension as $\mathbf{c}$, and thus is normal to $S$. A tangent plane can then be
described via the same technique above.

\subsection{Chain Rule for Multivariable Functions}
\newthought{If} $f$ is a function of $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, and $x_i$ is a function of $t$, then:
\[\dfrac{df}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\dfrac{dx_i}{dt}\]
Alternatively, if $x_i$ are functions of $s$ and $t$, then:
\begin{align*}
  \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial s} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\dfrac{\partial x_i}{\partial s} \\
  \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\dfrac{\partial x_i}{\partial t}
\end{align*}

\subsection{The Jacobian Matrix}
\newthought{If} $\mathbf{F}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, then:
\[
  \mathbf{F}(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    f_1(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_m) \\
    f_2(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_m) \\
    \vdots \\
    f_n(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_m)
  \end{bmatrix}
\]
The derivative of $\mathbf{F}$ is then an $n \times m$ matrix, called the Jacobian matrix made up of all
partial derivatives of
$\mathbf{F}$:
\[
  \mathbf{F}' =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial
    f_1}{\partial x_m} \\
    \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial
    f_2}{\partial x_m} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    \dfrac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_1} & \dfrac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial
    f_n}{\partial x_m}
  \end{bmatrix}
\]

If $(x,y,z) = \mathbf{F}(u,v,w)$ describes a coordinate transformation from $(x,y,z)$ to $(u,v,w)$, then the
determinant of the Jacobian matrix, called the Jacobian, is a scaling factor needed when changing coordinate systems
in integration, described further in Chapter 7. For example:
\begin{align*}
  (x,y) = \mathbf{F}(r,\theta) &= (r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta) \\
  \implies \mathbf{F}'(r,\theta) &=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\
    \sin\theta & r\cos\theta
  \end{bmatrix}\\
  |\mathbf{F}'| &= r
\end{align*}
So,
\[\int_{-r}^{r} \int_{-\sqrt{r-x^2}}^{\sqrt{r-x^2}}1 dy dx = \int_{0}^{r} \int_{0}^{2\pi}rd\theta dr = \pi r^2\]
\section{Maxima and Minima}
\newthought{Maxima and minima} are, as the name suggests, points of maximum and minimum. A point $a$ is
a maximum of $f$ if for every $c$, $f(c) \le f(a)$, and vice versa for minima.

A point $c$ is a \emph{critical point} of $f$ if $f'(c) = 0$. A critical point is a maximum if
$f''(c) < 0$ and a minimum if $f''(c) > 0$.

\newthought{For multivariable functions} we must instead look at the gradient vector. A point $\mathbf{c}$ is a critical
point of a function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ if $\Delta f(\mathbf{c})$ doesn't exist, or $\Delta
f(\mathbf{c}) = 0$. Define the Hessian matrix to be the square matrix of $n$ $2nd$ order partial derivatives of $f$:
\[
  D^2 f(\mathbf{c}) =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    f_{xx} & f_{xy} & \cdots & f_{xn} \\
    f_{yx} & f_{yy} & \cdots & f_{yn} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    f_{nx} & f_{ny} & \cdots & f_{nn}
  \end{bmatrix}
\]
Then if this matrix exists and $\Delta f(\mathbf{c}) = 0$ (or dne), the determinant $|D^2 f(\mathbf{c})|$,
denoted $D_f(\mathbf{c})$ provides the information for the multivariable equivalent of the second derivative
test.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $D_f(\mathbf{c}) > 0$ and $f_{xx} > 0$ then $f$ has a local minimum at \textbf{c}
  \item If $D_f(\mathbf{c}) > 0$ and $f_{xx} < 0$ then $f$ has a local maximum at \textbf{c}
  \item If $D_f(\mathbf{c}) < 0$ then $f$ has a saddle point at \textbf{c}
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Lagrange Multipliers}
\newthought{Let $f(\mathbf{x})$ and $g(\mathbf{x})$ be continuously differentiable scalar functions on
  $\mathbb{R}^n$. If $f$ has a local extremum at \textbf{c}, subject to a constraint $g(\mathbf{x})=k$, for $k
  \in \mathbb{R}$, and $\Delta g(\mathbf{c}) \ne 0$, then $\exists \lambda$ such that $\Delta f(\mathbf{c}) =
\lambda\Delta g(\mathbf{c})$.} This $\lambda$ is known as the Lagrange multiplier.

\begin{wrapfigure}{o}{0.4\textwidth}
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.95\linewidth}{!}{%
    \begin{tikzpicture}[line width=1pt]
      \fill[pattern=north east lines] (-2.5,-0.1) rectangle (2.5,0);
      \draw (-2.5,0) -- (2.5,0);
      \draw (-1.5,0) rectangle (1.5,2.2);

      \node at (-1.9,1.1) {\small $x$};

      \node at (0,1.1) {\small $\text{Area} = A = xy$};
    \end{tikzpicture}%
  }
  \caption{The farmer's pen}
  \label{fig:pen}
\end{wrapfigure}
A farmer has 200 metres of fencing with which to construct three sides of a rectangular pen, with an existing
fence acting as the fourth side (\cref{fig:pen}) What dimensions will maximise the area of the pen?

This obviously leads to the system of linear equations:
\begin{align*}
  A(x,y) &= xy \\
  P(x,y) &= 2x+y
\end{align*}
However, $P(x,y)$ is subject to the constraint that $P(x,y)=200$. So, we have $\Delta A(x,y) = (y,x)$ and
$\Delta P(x,y)=(2,1)$. We arrive at a system of three equations in three unknowns
\begin{align*}
  2x+y &= 200 \\
  y-2\lambda &= 0\\
  x-\lambda &= 0
\end{align*}
which corresponds to the augmented matrix
\[
  \left[
    \begin{array}{ccc|c}
      2 & 1 & 0 & 200 \\
      0 & 1 & -2 & 0 \\
      1 & 0 & -1 & 0
    \end{array}
  \right]
\]
which reduces to\footnote{\ refer: MATH1012 Mathematical Theory and Methods}
\[
  \left[
    \begin{array}{ccc|c}
      1 & 0 & 0 & 50 \\
      0 & 1 & 0 & 100 \\
      0 & 0 & 1 & 50
    \end{array}
  \right]
\]
Thus, $x=50$ and $y=100$. This obviously was a trivial problem that could have been solved by a simple
substitution, however the method of Lagrange multipliers proves useful in more complicated optimisation problems.

\section{Taylor Series}
\newthought{It is often convenient} to approximate a function by a polynomial; if it is too complicated to integrate,
for example. Define
\[T_{n,a}(x) = f(a) + \dfrac{f'(a)}{1!}(x-a) + \dfrac{f''(a)}{2!}{(x-a)}^2 + \cdots + \dfrac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}{(x-a)}^n\]
To be the \emph{Taylor Polynomial} of $n'th$ degree of $f$ around $a$. Then $f(x) = T_{n,a}(x)+R_{n,a}(x)$,
where $R_{n,a}(x)$ is an \emph{error term} or \emph{remainder}. For example, the Taylor polynomial of $\sin x$
around 0:
\[T_{6,0}(x) = x - \dfrac{x^3}{6} + \dfrac{x^5}{120} + R_{6,0}(x)\]
The formular for $R_{n,a}(x)$ is given by
\[R_{n,a}(x) = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(z)}{(n+1)!}{(x-a)}^{n+1}\]
for some $z \in [a,x]$. We do not know $z$, however it is possible to bound the error and thus know how
accurate our polynomial expansions are\footnote{\ refer: MATH1012 Mathematical Theory and Methods.}. For the
expansion of $\sin x$ around 0,
\[R_{6,0} = \dfrac{f^{(7)}(z)}{7!}x^7\quad z \in [0,x]\]
Lets say that we want to estimate $\sin(\pi/4)$:
\[|R_{6,0}(x)| = \dfrac{|\cos z|}{5040} |x|^7 \le \dfrac{1}{5040}\left|\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right|^7 = 3.66\times 10^{-5}\]
so we can be quite comfortable using our Taylor expansion up to 4 decimal places of $\sin (\pi/4)$. In fact
\[\sin(\pi/4) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.707107 \quad \text{and} \quad R_{6,0}(\pi/4) = 0.707143\]
