\section{Lecture 1}
\subsection{Double and Triple Integrals}
\newthought{To integrate} a scalar function $z = f(x,y)$ over a bounded region, we can take either horizontal or vertical slices,
and sum them. For example, to calculate $\iint (f(x,y)=xy) \,dA $ over the region bound by $y=\sqrt{x}$ and $y=x^3$ (\cref{fig:doule-integral-fig1}).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \begin{axis}[
        axis lines = middle,
        xlabel = $x$,
        ylabel = $y$,
        xtick = {1},
        ytick = {1},
        xticklabels = {1},
        yticklabels = {1},
        xmin = -0.1, xmax = 1.3,
        ymin = -0.1, ymax = 1.3,
        axis equal image,
        samples = 100,
      ]

      % Curves
      \addplot[name path = upper, domain=0:1.3, black, thick] {sqrt(x)};
      \addplot[name path = lower, domain=0:1.3, black, thick] {x^3};

      \node[black] at (axis cs:0.8,1.1) {$y=\sqrt{x}$};
      \node[black]  at (axis cs:1.1,0.8) {$y=x^3$};

      % Shaded region between the curves from x=0 to x=1
      \addplot[
        fill = red!25,
        draw = none,
      ] fill between[
        of = upper and lower,
        soft clip = {domain=0:1},
      ];

    \end{axis}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{The region bound by $y=\sqrt{x}$ and $y=x^3$.}
  \label{fig:doule-integral-fig1}
\end{figure}
We can either take vertical slices (i.e take regular portions of $x$ and integrate w.r.t $y$ first),
\[
  \int_{0}^{1} \int_{x^3}^{\sqrt{x}} (xy)  \,dy \,dx = \int_{0}^{1} \left[ \dfrac{xy^2}{2} \right]_{x^3}^{\sqrt{x}} \,dx
\]
\[
  = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{1} x^2 - x^7 \,dx = \dfrac{1}{2} \left[ \dfrac{x^3}{3} - \dfrac{x^8}{8} \right]_{0}^{1}
\]
\[
  = \dfrac{1}{2} \left( \dfrac{1}{3} - \dfrac {1}{8} \right) = \dfrac{5}{48}
\]
Or we can take horizontal slices (i.e take regular portions of $y$ and integrate w.r.t $x$ first),
\[
  \int_{0}^{1} \int^{x=y^{1/3}}_{x=y^2} (xy) \,dx \,dy = \int_{0}^{1} \left[ \dfrac{x^2y}{2} \right]^{y^{1/3}}_{y^2} \,dy
\]
\[
  = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{1} y^{5/3} - y^5 \,dy = \dfrac{1}{2} \left[ \dfrac{3y^{8/3}}{8} - \dfrac{y^6}{6} \right]_{0}^{1}
\]
\[
  = \dfrac{1}{2} \left( \dfrac{3}{8} - \dfrac{1}{6} \right) = \dfrac{5}{48}
\]
Both approaches yield the same result, but one may be easier than the other. For example,
\[\int_{y=0}^{y=1} \int_{x=\sqrt{y}}^{x=1} e^{x^3} \,dx \,dy\]
is impossible, but reversing the order of integration makes it doable.
\[\int_{x=0}^{x=1} \int_{y=0}^{y=x^2} e^{x^3} \,dy \,dx \ = \int_{0}^{1} \left[ e^{x^3}y \right]^{x^2}_0 \,dx \]
\[\text{let }u=x^3 : \int_{0}^{1} x^2e^{x^3} \,dx = \dfrac{1}{3} \int_{0}^{1} e^u \,du\]
\[= \dfrac{1}{3} \left[ e^u \right]^{1}_{0} = \dfrac{1}{3}(e-1)\]

\newthought{Triple integrals} are solved in much the same way, as a serious of single integrals.
\[\iiint f(x,y,z) \,dV : a \le x \le b,\ q(x) \le y \le w(x),\ g(x,y) \le z \le h(x,y)\]
\[= \int_{a}^{b} \left(\int_{q(x)}^{w(x)} \left(\int_{g(x,y)}^{h(x,y)} f(x,y,z) \,dz\right) \,dy\right) \,dx\]

To find the volume of a region $R$, integrate the function $f(x,y,z)=1$.

\subsection{Changing Variables in Double and Triple Integrals}
\newthought{To change} from $(x,y)$ to $(u,v)$, you first need to define a transformation $\mathbf{g}(u,v) = (x,y)$. A common
transformation is from $(x,y)$ to $(r,\theta)$, with $r$ being the distance from the origin, and $\theta$ being the angle from an
arbitrary axis.
\[\mathbf{g}(r,\theta) =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    r \cos \theta \\ r \sin \theta
  \end{bmatrix} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x \\ y
\end{bmatrix}\]
Then, define the \emph{Jacobian} matrix to be:
\[\mathbf{J} = \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{g}(u,v)}{\partial(u,v)} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\
    \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}
\end{bmatrix}\]
For the polar coordinates transformation,
\[\mathbf{J} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial r} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} \\
    \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial r} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}
  \end{bmatrix} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \cos \theta & -r \sin \theta \\
    \sin \theta & r \cos \theta
\end{bmatrix}\]
Then, \[\iint f(x,y) \,dx \,dy = \iint f(x(u,v),y(u,v))|\mathbf{J}|\,du \,dy\]
For polar coordinates,
\[|\mathbf{J}|=r \cos^2 \theta + r \sin^2 \theta = r\]
So,
\[\iint f(x,y)\,dx\,dy = \iint f(r,\theta)r\,dr\,d\theta\]
The process is the same for triple integrals, except that calculating the determinant by hand is slightly more annoying.
When \[x=x(u,v,w),\ y=y(u,v,w),\ z=z(u,v,w)\],
\[\mathbf{J} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial w} \\
    \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial w} \\
    \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial z} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial z} & \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w} \\
\end{bmatrix}\]
\subsection{Polar Coordinates}
As discussed above, polar coordinates involves expressing points on the Cartesian plane in terms of $r$ and $\theta$,
where $r$ is the distance from the origin, and $\theta$ is the angle from an axis, usually the $x$-axis. Switching from
$(x,y) \to (r,\theta)$ is usually beneficial when the problem being solved is symmetric about rotation around some point.

For example, switch from $(x,y) \to (r,\theta)$ to solve $\iint_{R} \sin(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy$ where $R$ is the region
$1 \le x^2+y^2 \le 16$ (\cref{fig:plot_of_r}).
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.6]

    % Shaded annulus: 1 <= x^2+y^2 <= 16, i.e. radius between 1 and 4

    (0,0) circle (4)
    (0,0) circle (1);

    % Axes
    \draw[->] (-4.7,0) -- (4.7,0) node[right] {$x$};
    \draw[->] (0,-4.7) -- (0,4.7) node[above] {$y$};

    % Circle boundaries
    \draw[thick, black] (0,0) circle (4);
    \draw[thick, black] (0,0) circle (1);

    % Radius labels
    \draw[dashed, gray] (0,0) -- (1,0);
    \draw[dashed, gray] (0,0) -- (4,0);
    \node[below right] at (1,0) {$1$};
    \node[below right] at (4,0) {$4$};

  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Plot of $R$.}
  \label{fig:plot_of_r}
\end{figure}
\[
  \iint_{R} \sin(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{1}^{4}r \sin(r^2) \,dr\,d\theta
\]
\[
  \text{let }u=r^2,\ \,du = 2r \,dr : \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{1}^{4}r \sin(r^2) \,dr\,d\theta = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{1}^{16} \sin(u) \,du\,d\theta
\]
\[
  = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left[-\cos(u) \right]^{16}_{1} \,d\theta = \left[ \left(\cos(1)-\cos(16)\right)\theta\right]^{2\pi}_{0} \approx 9.4
\]
\subsection{Cylindrical Polar Coordinates}

The point $P=(x,y,z)$ is projected down to the $x,y$-plane and then converted to polar coordinates; $P(x,y,0) \to P(r,\theta,0)$.
The $z$-coordinate is then reused. Cylindrical polar coordinates are best used for problems that are symmetric about rotation around
the $z$-axis. Since this is essentially polar coordinates, the determinant of the Jacobian is $r$.

For example, the mass of a solid cylinder $C$ of radius 1, and height 2, centered at the origin (\cref{fig:cylinder}) has density
\[D(x,y,z)=1+x^2+y^2+z^2\]

To find the mass of this cylinder, we need to solve $\iiint_{C}D(x,y,z)\,dV$
\[\iiint_{C}D(x,y,z)\,dV = \iiint_{C'}D(r,\theta,z)r\,dr \,d\theta \,dz\]
\[= \int_{-1}^{1} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} r(1+r^2+z^2) \,dr \,d\theta \,dz\]
\[= \int_{-1}^{1} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left[ \dfrac{r^2}{2} + \dfrac{r^4}{4} + \dfrac{r^2}{2}z^2 \right]^{1}_{0} \,d\theta \,dz = \int_{-1}^{1} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \dfrac{3}{4} + \dfrac{1}{2}z^2 \,d\theta \,dz\]
\[= \int_{-1}^{1} \left[ \dfrac{3}{4}\theta + \dfrac{1}{2}z^2\theta \right]^{2\pi}_{0} \,dz = \int_{-1}^{1} \dfrac{6\pi}{4} + \pi z^2 \,dz\]
\[= \left[ \dfrac{6\pi}{4}z + \dfrac{\pi}{3}z^3 \right]^{1}_{-1} = \dfrac{11\pi}{3}\]

\subsection{Spherical Polar Coordinates}
\newthought{Spherical Polar Coordinates} involves changing from $(x,y,z) \to (r,\theta,\phi)$, where $r$ is the distance from the
point to the origin, $\theta$ is the projected angle from the $x$-axis (the same as cylindrical polars), and $\phi$ is the angle
from the $z$-axis to the point projected onto the $x,z$-plane (taking $y=0$) (\cref{fig:spherical}). The determinant of the Jacobian
for spherical polar coordinates is $r^2 \sin\phi$.

The mass of a sphere of radius 1 centered at the origin with density
\[D(x,y,z)=1+x^2+y^2+z^2=1+r^2\]
is
\[\int^{\pi}_{0} \int^{2\pi}_{0} \int^{1}_{0} (1+r^2)(r^2 \sin \phi) \,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi\]
\[= \int^{\pi}_{0} \int^{2\pi}_{0} \left[ \dfrac{r^3}{3}+\dfrac{r^5}{5} \right]^{1}_{0} \sin \phi \,d\theta \,d\phi\]
\[= \dfrac{8}{15} \int_{0}^{\pi} 2\pi \sin \phi \,d\phi = \dfrac{16\pi}{15} \left[ -\cos\phi \right]^{\pi}_{0} = \dfrac{32\pi}{15}\]
